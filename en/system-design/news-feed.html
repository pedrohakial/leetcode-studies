News Feed

Category: fan-out / social graph · Difficulty: classic — the feed question is to social what TinyURL is to key-value: the interviewer wants to hear your fan-out reasoning with the number on the tip of your tongue.

1. Requirements

Functional:

  • Feed with posts from people you follow, newest first (recency; simple ranking is a later evolution)
  • Publish a post; follow/unfollow; infinite pagination (scroll)
  • Out of scope for the 1st iteration: comments, reactions, ads, algorithm

Non-functional:

  • Very heavy reads (the feed is the product’s hottest path — every user opens it 10–20×/day); light writes (1–2 posts/day)
  • Feed-open latency < 500 ms
  • Eventual consistency acceptable: a published post may take seconds to reach feeds; the post itself needs immediate durability
  • Availability > 99.9% — a wall of shame on the home page is brand damage

Estimates (back-of-the-napkin):

  • 200 M DAU × 10 feed loads/day = 2 B loads/day ÷ 86,400 ≈ ~23k loads/s average, peak 2–3× ≈ 70k/s (each load returns ~100–200 posts → 2–4 M cache reads/s)
  • Posts: 200 M × 2/day = 400 M/day ÷ 86,400 ≈ 4.6k posts/s of writes
  • Materialized feed: 200 M users × 100 ids × 8 B ≈ 160 GB in Redis (cluster, 24 h TTL)
  • On-write fan-out: 4.6k/s × mean 200 followers ≈ ~1 M feed-writes/s async — and a celebrity with 10 M followers is 10 M writes for a single post: the number that kills pure on-write

2. API + data model

POST /posts                    {text, media_uri?}         → {post_id}
GET  /feed?cursor=<opaque>&limit=20
                              → {posts: [{id, author, text, created_at}],
                                 next_cursor}
POST/DELETE /users/{id}/follow

users(id, name)
posts(id BIGINT UNIQUE, author_id, text, created_at)   -- shard by id
followers(follower_id, followed_id,
          PK (follower_id, followed_id))               -- shard by follower
-- materialized feed (Redis in practice; the SQL is the design):
feed_cache(user_id, post_id, score,
           PK (user_id, post_id))                      -- shard by user

A monotonic post_id (snowflake: time + node + seq) is what makes the feed sortable and the cursor trivial — uuid v4 doesn’t order, and order is the requirement.

3. High level

client → LB → feed service (stateless)
                 │
        feed cache (Redis: pages of post-ids per user)
                 │ miss
                 ▼
          posts/followers service → sharded DB
                 ▲
          fan-out queue → feed-materializing workers
  • Feed service: assembles the page from the cache; on a miss, queries the post/followers services and refills.
  • Redis: every user has their own list of post-ids (the page is pre-resolved — hot reads never touch the DB).
  • Fan-out queue + workers: the fan-out write spike must not stall publishing; process in batches and in order.
  • Sharded DB: posts and the follower graph are the durable truth; shard by post_id/follower_id for each load’s queries.

4. Deep dive

a) Fan-out: on-write vs on-read vs hybrid (the heart)

  • On-write: the published post goes to a queue; a worker copies the post_id into the feed_cache of every follower. Read = 1 Redis lookup (fast as hell). Write = O(followers) — and a 10 M celebrity blows up the queue and Redis on a single post.
  • On-read: nothing is materialized; on read, the feed fetches the latest posts of each followed user (N queries) and merges in sorted order. Write O(1), read O(followed) — 1,000 follows = 1,000 queries per load; great for people who follow little, terrible for power users (and power users live in the feed).
  • Hybrid (the senior answer):
    • users with few followers (the overwhelming majority) → on-write;
    • celebrities (a clear threshold, e.g., > 100k followers) → flagged at follow time; their posts stay a single copy;
    • on read, the feed = merge of the materialized feed + live pull of the celebrity posts that user follows (and each user follows few celebrities — few queries).
    • Result: the expensive write (10 M writes) becomes 1 write + a cheap pull per reader. The 1% of users generate the disproportionate share of writes; that’s the slice that leaves on-write.
  • The golden rule to memorize: writing once and pulling on read is always cheaper than writing N copies when N is large; the threshold decides where “large” starts.

b) Feed cache, invalidation, and hot keys

  • What’s cached: the user’s list of post-ids (not the posts — those come from a shared post cache, deduplicated across feeds). TTL 24 h + LRU eviction by usage.
  • Invalidation is rewrite, not delete: fan-out doesn’t delete — it appends the new post to the top of the list and evicts the oldest; event-based invalidation (post edit/removal) is rare and can be best-effort (a removed post disappears on the next page assembly).
  • Hot keys: a famous user reading constantly = the same hot Redis key → 2-layer cache (per-node local LRU + Redis) for the 1% mega-hot feeds; thundering herd on miss → a single placeholder that assembles the page and the rest wait on the same (distributed lock), instead of 1,000 identical queries.
  • The room number: 160 GB of feed cache + posts (plus duplication) — the Redis cluster is the physical bottleneck; the real bottlenecks are in item 5.

c) Cursor pagination vs offset + basic ranking

  • Cursor = post_id (opaque to the client): stable even when new posts arrive during the scroll — no duplicates, no skips. Offset breaks: a new post shifts the indices and the next page repeats an item (or skips one) — demonstrated below.
# (tested) cursor walks everything without duplicates; a post arriving in
# the middle of pagination does NOT enter the in-flight sequence (nor shifts
# it).
def page(feed, cursor, limit):
    remaining = feed if cursor is None else [p for p in feed if p[0] < cursor]
    chosen = remaining[:limit]
    next_ = chosen[-1][0] if chosen else None
    return chosen, next_

feed = [(10, "a"), (9, "b"), (8, "c"), (7, "d"), (6, "e")]
cur, collected = None, []
while True:
    slice_, cur = page(feed, cur, limit=2)
    if not slice_:
        break
    collected.extend(f[0] for f in slice_)
assert collected == [10, 9, 8, 7, 6]          # walked everything, no dups

# post 11 arrives BETWEEN pages: the old sequence stays intact
feed2 = [(10, "a"), (9, "b"), (8, "c"), (7, "d"), (6, "e")]
cur, collected = None, []
while True:
    slice_, cur = page(feed2, cur, limit=2)
    if not slice_:
        break
    collected.extend(f[0] for f in slice_)
    feed2.insert(0, (11, "new"))
assert collected == [10, 9, 8, 7, 6]          # stable: nothing dup/skips

# offset with a new post mid-stream → repeated page (here, 9 in both):
feed = [(10, "a"), (9, "b"), (8, "c"), (7, "d"), (6, "e")]
seen = [f[0] for f in feed[0:2]]              # page 1: 10, 9
feed.insert(0, (11, "new"))
seen += [f[0] for f in feed[2:4]]             # page 2: 9, 8  ← 9 repeated
seen += [f[0] for f in feed[4:6]]             # 7, 6
assert seen == [10, 9, 9, 8, 7, 6], seen       # duplicate: proven
  • Basic ranking: pure recency first (chronological — the id cursor already covers it). Evolution: score = f(age, estimated affinity, engagement) — and that changes the cache strategy: don’t cache the final order, cache the candidates and rank the page on read (100–200 items is cheap to sort in memory); a periodic job re-ranks the top of hot feeds.

5. Trade-offs and bottlenecks

  • Breaks first: the feed Redis (160 GB + hot keys + ~1 M/s fan-out writes) and the posts DB on writes (4.6k/s × post size + media via CDN with signed upload).
  • Conscious sacrifice: eventual consistency (a post takes seconds to arrive — fine); live pull for celebrity followers (slightly more expensive reads, much cheaper writes); feed cache with TTL (an inactive user’s “erased” feed is rebuilt on the next load).
  • With 10× more time: real ranking (ML: affinity, recency, content type), ads in the feed (real-time auction — then latency becomes a different-level requirement), memories/long-tail (old content needs a separate time-based index), per-user blocklists/filters, geo and moderation (a flagging queue removing posts from feeds via rewrite), media embedding.
  • Common bonus question: “what about a deleted post?” → a removal event walks the queue (fair), but the feed is eventual: visible removals are a fast-path (a removed-posts table checked at assembly, or a priority queue message) — mention that removing abusive content can’t wait for the fan-out tail.
